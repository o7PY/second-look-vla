Second Look

Part 4 · Steering the retry noise

One retry differs from another only by luck. Can we pick good luck?

Between two retries from the same moment, the only thing that differs is the random noise its policy starts from (every retry also pauses and rewinds the arm the same way). If some draws are much better than others, perhaps we can learn to choose them. We asked first whether that room exists, then whether anything available at retry time reveals it. No retraining or reinforcement learning was done.

Step 1: does the room exist?

We took 40 failed situations from SmolVLA runs in simulation and, for each, tried 64 different noise draws for the retry. Slide to see what an all-seeing picker would achieve if it could try that many draws and keep the best.

of failures rescued

Each dot is one failed situation. The dots show the share rescued, not which ones.

One random retry rescues 28.3%. The best of 64 would rescue 60.0%, a gap of +31.7 pp. This was a pre-registered go/no-go test, and the answer is go: the room exists.

Treat the best-of-64 figure as an upper bound. It assumes a picker that already knows which draw will win, and taking the best of many draws inflates the result.

The same test on the 20 situations where the run would have succeeded anyway: one random retry succeeds only 58.1% of the time, so retrying every alarm puts runs that were fine at risk.

Step 2: can anyone see the good draw?

A perfect picker is not available, so we trained a critic that looks at observation, noise and plan and tries to rank a situation’s good draws above its bad ones. A score of 0.5 is chance. We had written down in advance that it needed 0.65 to count as useful.

What the critic is

A critic scores each noise draw: how likely is a retry that starts from this noise to succeed? At retry time the robot would sample a handful of draws (we used 8), score them all, and keep the best.

1 · The situation28 numbers from the alarm: the task, alarm signals, gripper state and DeltaDINO’s score.
+
2 · The planWhat SmolVLA would do from this draw: its action chunk (50×7 = 350 numbers), plus summaries such as acceleration, how far the arm moves, and how different this plan is from the other draws.
+
3 · The noiseThe 50×32 = 1,600 numbers the plan starts from, plus their overall size.
→
4 · The scorerA regularised logistic regression on standardised inputs. It outputs one score per draw.

How to choose the scorer was fixed in advance: an inner cross-validation picked among three candidates (logistic regression on situation and plan, boosted trees on the same, and logistic regression on situation, plan and noise), and it chose logistic regression on situation, plan and noise in 48 of 48 folds. That is the scorer evaluated below. It was always tested on data it had never seen: one task-and-starting-layout group was held out at a time.

Where it comes from

  • The idea. Steering a frozen policy by choosing its starting noise comes from DSRL (Wagenmaker et al., CoRL 2025), where a small learned actor picks the noise. We did not build that. We asked the cheaper question first: can a critic tell good noise from bad?
  • The model. Our own design from standard tools: scikit-learn logistic regression and gradient-boosted trees. No neural network was trained.
  • The ingredients. The situation features reuse the inputs of the retry selector from the previous part. The plan is SmolVLA’s own output. The acceleration summary is the same score our alarm uses, ported from the open-source fm-geometry code (Apache-2.0).
  • Not tried. A learned value head on the policy’s own internal features, trained end to end.

The pale band is the 95% confidence interval for our critic.

The critic scored 0.552[0.515, 0.592]. Picking its favourite of 8 draws gained only +2.4 pp over a random retry[−0.5, +5.3]. That is a no-go.

What we learned

  • The room exists, but we could not find a way to see it. A perfect noise picker would help a lot. A critic using the situation, the plan and the noise ranked draws only slightly better than chance (AUROC 0.552 [0.515, 0.592], an interval that excludes 0.5; a post hoc permutation test gives p = 0.0100), far too weak to pick better draws (+2.4 pp over a random retry, not significant).
  • So a noise-steering policy would have to discover the signal online, by trial, instead of reading it off the situation.
  • The first noise matters, unevenly. Across 10 situations that sometimes succeed and sometimes fail, keeping the first noise from a draw that had succeeded (and resampling all later noise) raised success from 43.1% to 61.9%: +18.8 pp on average [+3.1, +39.1]. It was positive in 6 situations, negative in 2 and flat in 2. By the pre-registered rule (20 points or more is strong, under 10 weak) this is a mixed result.
