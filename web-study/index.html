    Second Look, explained from the ground up

    This page teaches every experiment on the Second Look site: what was done, why, how, the background you need first, and every setting that matters. It reads like a course module.     The story in six sentences

    1. A robot controlled by an AI policy (SmolVLASmolVLA. A small open-source VLA policy. Here: the checkpoint HuggingFaceVLA/smolvla_libero, used frozen and never retrained.) sometimes fails halfway through a task. The obvious fix is to try again.
    2. But if the failure moved something (a bowl is now on the floor), trying again from the old plan can make things worse.
    3. So we built an alarm that notices the policy getting shaky, and a camera check (DeltaDINODeltaDINO. The camera check: a LoRA-tuned DINOv2-small that compares a before and an after image and says whether any object moved.) that compares the scene before and now.
    4. Part 1 shows the camera check works: it tells whether anything moved, from two photos.
    5. Part 2 puts everything into a loop (alarm, pause, look, retry or flag a human) and finds it makes failures visible but does not make the robot succeed more.
    6. Part 3 asks whether a learned model can choose when a retry is worth it (a little, but no better than always retrying), and Part 4 asks whether the retry's random noise can be steered (room exists, but nothing we tried could see it).

    2.7 Three special datasets: calibration, the 218 cases, the 40 states

    The alarm's calibration data (Part 2)

    200 SmolVLA episodes with no intervention (P0), init 40-49, 42.9 minutes. Of these, 121 were successes (the alarm's false-alarm rate on them was 9.1%) and 79 were failures (the catch rate below is over these). The CUSUM's reference level and spread come from the successful episodes, its height from the split-conformal rule on their peaks, and the result was frozen in configs/part2_frozen.yaml before any DEV episode. A second calibration run of gate thresholds (per rewind distance) used the P2.3b stage-check pairs from CAL episodes, seen tasks only.

    The 218 branched cases (Part 3)

    1. Run SmolVLA with no intervention on the 600 GEN episodes (init 0-19, 3 seeds, 10 tasks).
    2. Whenever the accel alarm fires, take a snapshot at the first alarm, after the 10-step pause.
    3. From that snapshot run 4 retry branches (rewind the arm 6 decisions, one retry with fresh noise; noise index r = 1..4) and 4 continue branches (carry on with the plan already made; noise index 2000 + c).
    4. Per case: p_retry = successes / 4 and p_continue = successes / 4. Cases come from failures and from false alarms on successful episodes.

    Outcome: 218 labelled cases in 114 (task, init) groups; 374 episodes had no alarm and 8 ended inside the pause. The plan asked for 4 seeds; the benchmark projected 13.8 h for 4 seeds and 10.4 h for 3, so by the spec's own rule GEN ran 3 seeds (it took 5 h 40 min).     The 40 noise-ceiling states (Part 4)

    1. Candidates: the first accel alarm of each Part 3 GEN no-intervention episode whose alarm reached its decision. Population A = those episodes that failed; population C = those that succeeded (a baseline for harm).
    2. Selection: stratified by task, capped at 8 per task, sampled with numpy default_rng(0) over the sorted (task, init, seed) list. The plan said up to 60 A states.
    3.     4. Each state is rebuilt by replaying the logged episode from reset; its decisions up to the alarm must be bit-for-bit equal to the GEN log or the state is dropped.
    5. The 40 A states fall in 35 (task, init) clusters; 20 C states were also run (20).

    2.8 Determinism and seeding

    Every random draw is keyed noisekeyed noise. Noise computed from a hash of where it is used (split, task, init, seed, intervention, decision), not drawn from a running random generator. Makes every run replayable bit for bit., not streamed: sha256(split | task | init | seed label | intervention index | decision | k), where k = 0 is the executed chunk and k = 1..8 are resamples used by the STAC score. The arm name is deliberately not in the key, so all arms see identical noise at every decision until the first one acts. Why this matters:

    • Paired arms. A difference between arms is caused by the intervention, not by luck.
    • Replay. Any episode can be re-run exactly. This is tested: Part 2's gate G2 compared 10 branched episodes with fresh replays bit for bit; Part 4 replays every state to its alarm and requires equal decisions.
    • New experiments never disturb old keys. Each part adds its own namespace (Part 4 uses p5nc<j> and p5nc1b_<j>_<m>), and a test asserts that no existing key changes.
    • Regression on every code change. Additive changes to the shared loop are checked by replaying stored episodes ("Z1": 6 Part 2 and 6 Part 3 episodes, every arm equal to its stored log).
    The render-context bug that determinism found

    With more than one LIBERO environment alive in a process, a late-destroyed render context deleted the current environment's GL objects: from the second episode of a process on, the policy saw garbage images and failed. It was found by a live test, fixed in glfix.py with a regression test, and an image-sanity assertion now crashes an episode on a degenerate camera image. A re-render audit then found 70 of 70 stored pairs bitwise equal in fresh single-environment processes, so Part 1's flows were unaffected. Source: results/part2/notes/deviations.md, 2026-10-10.

    The budget clock

    elapsed = executed steps + pause steps + return-cost steps, and it never goes backwards: a rewind restores the arm, not the clock. A late alarm may leave too few steps to pay for a rewind (four of the Part 4 failed-alarm states were exactly that case, and a fifth state hit an invalid rewind). Source: context/site/src/pages/how-it-works.md §1; budget.py.

    b. Background

    This is the first experiment, so it builds on no earlier part. It rests on a policy that fails often enough to matter: SmolVLA completes about two of three LIBERO-Goal episodes. A retry is only safe if the scene is still the one the robot planned for; if the bowl fell off the plate, rewinding the arm is the wrong move and a person should be told. So the loop needs a camera check before anything else.

    Related work named in the project docs: the alarm's proxy comes from fm-geometry ("denoising acceleration"); SAFE and FIPER are other calibrated failure detectors; Rewind-IL is the closest rewind-on-signal system. All were read at abstract level only, so no comparison is claimed. The project believes a two-photo (Siamese) comparison that decides between retrying and calling a human is new, but a full literature check was not done. Source: context/site/src/pages/related-work.md.

    c. Why we did it

    The question: can a small network look at a reference frame and a settled frame and tell, from pixels alone, whether any object in the scene moved? And are its good numbers real, meaning does it respond to the visible change, or to artefacts of how the data was built? The second question became Part 1.5 and the "before Part 2" checks.

    f. What it means, and what it does not show

    It means: a small fine-tuned vision model can tell whether objects moved, in this simulator, on tasks it saw, at high accuracy; the arm itself does not drive its decisions; and the checks found real problems (a spurious background patch, labels that cannot be seen, a hidden cost of masking, a stale render) and fixed two of them.

    It does not show:

    • Anything about tasks it has not seen (held-out recall on big changes is lower).
    • That blind spots are harmless: an object lying entirely inside the static region is untested (the probe has only 2 such positives); changes under the arm cannot be erased for the removal test; small rotations and knob turns are largely invisible at 224 pixels.
    • That a human reviewer agrees with the categories. R8, a blind 40-row human review, is pending; the sheet is prepared and nothing is scored.

    g. Check yourself

    1. A detector has AUROC 0.99 but flags 10% of unchanged scenes. What does that say about its ranking and its threshold?

    The ranking is excellent (almost every changed pair outscores almost every unchanged pair), but the threshold is set too low for this population. AUROC is threshold-free. Part 2 hit exactly this: the gate's AUROC stayed high while its false-positive rate at the old threshold was 7.1% to 11.9%.

    2. Why are hard negatives (arm-only, occluder, jitter, static) in the dataset?

    Without them a model could learn "the arm is near an object, so something changed". Hard negatives look changed (the arm moves, the arm hides an object) but nothing moved, so the model must look at the objects.

    3. Why are the labels computed from simulator poses and not from what the generator was trying to do?

    A push can fail to move an object, or a "static" pair can contain a tiny drift. Intent can be wrong; measurement cannot. Pairs whose measured label disagrees with the generator's purpose are dropped, and pairs in the 0.5-1 cm or 2.5-5 degree gray zone are kept only for testing.

    4. Why did the project set aside the rule's choice (model A) and use model C in Part 2?

    R6 picked A because only A passed all five gates; C missed the policy removal bar. The user overrode it: C's only failed gate is dominated by changes under the arm, which the removal test cannot erase, and on the fair stratum C passes. The override is recorded in p2_0_model_override.md. The sealed-set result (C: AUROC 0.993) was evaluated on both candidates in one pass.

    f. What it means, and what it does not show

    It means: a camera check plus a retry loop turns many silent failures into flagged ones with good precision (80.1%), at a cost of about three points of success.     It does not show: that success cannot be improved (a different flag rule is a different experiment); that the configuration is the best one (it was chosen by a fallback rule written after DEV was seen); that the held-out gain is real; anything beyond one policy, one benchmark and simulation (CAL and TEST share init states 40-49, though the clean 20-39 subset gives the same verdicts); or that the silent-failure metric is standard (it is the project's own and only means something next to flag precision).

    g. Check yourself

    Flagging every episode gives zero silent failures. So the metric is always read with flag precision (what share of flags were on episodes the baseline fails) and false flags (flags on episodes the baseline finishes). Here precision was 80.1%.

    2. The oracle gate (P4) scores the same as the camera gate (P3). What does that tell you?

    That a better camera would not help: the information "did something move" is already available to the loop. The weakness is in what to do with that answer, because moved objects are often the robot's own progress.

    3. H3 was supported. Does that mean Second Look beats doing nothing?

    No. The comparison arm (random-timed alarms) is itself harmful, so beating it only shows that the alarm's timing carries information.

    At alarm time the frames are different from Part 1's pairs; at Part 1's threshold the in-loop false-positive rate was 7.1% to 11.9%. AUROC stayed high, so ranking was fine and only the threshold was off. A threshold was fixed per rewind distance on CAL, no retraining.

    b. Background

    c. Why we did it

    f. What it means, and what it does not show

    It means: at this data size, the signals available at the first alarm tell you the task and little else. Calibration is good; discrimination beyond the task is not. A learned selector recovers the benefit of retrying while avoiding most of its harm, but so does a lookup table by task.

    It does not show: that retry value is unpredictable in principle (the model learned from 218 cases; a bigger set might find structure); that the camera score is useless in general; anything about new scenes (TEST is sealed by new noise seeds only); generalisation beyond one simulator, policy and benchmark; or that the gain over doing nothing is large.

    g. Check yourself

    1. SH beats doing nothing (H1) but not the task-id model (H3). What is the plain-language conclusion?

    The gain is task-level. Knowing which task you are on is enough to decide whether retrying tends to pay off; the alarm signals and camera score add nothing measurable.

    2. SH retried far less often than always-retry but ended with about the same success. How?

    It retried mostly where retries helped and skipped many where they hurt, so it avoided harms (18 against 42) while keeping fewer rescues (39 against 65). The net was similar. Calling it cautious rather than smart is the honest summary.

    3. Why must cross-validation be grouped by (task, init)?

    Cases from the same starting scene resemble each other. If they were split across train and test folds the score would reward memorising scenes and overstate how well the model generalises.

    4. What is the point of the "stop rule" before training?

    To avoid shipping a model that cannot beat the trivial policies. The model is only trained if its cross-validated decision value beats the better of always-retry and always-continue. It passed only narrowly, which already hints at the result.

    One retry differs from another only by luck. Can we pick good luck?

If some draws are much better than others, perhaps a system can learn to choose them. We asked first whether the room exists, then whether anything available at retry time reveals it. No retraining and no reinforcement learning were done.

    b. Background

    Part 2 showed a blind retry helps some alarms and hurts others. Part 3 tried to tell them apart from the alarm's signals and the camera and found only a small gain over always retrying: a plain retry rescued 65 test episodes and harmed 42, and the selector's gain over doing nothing was +2.1 pp. A retry changes only the fresh flow-matching noise, so the next question was about the noise itself.

    Where the idea comes from. Steering a frozen policy by choosing its starting noise comes from DSRL (Wagenmaker et al., CoRL 2025), where a small learned actor picks the noise by reinforcement learning. The project did not build that. It asked a cheaper question first: how much room does a perfect noise picker have, and can a critic tell good noise from bad? Related work named in the docs: V-GPS (value-guided re-ranking of sampled actions) and RoboMonkey (sample many, verify, pick one), both read at abstract level. Source: context/site/src/pages/related-work.md.

    c. Why we did it

    Three questions, each answered by a pre-registered rule: P5.0 (room?) how much better is the best of many noise draws than one random retry on alarmed failures; P5.1a (can it be seen?) using only what a deployed system knows, can a critic rank a situation's successful draws above its failed ones; P5.1b (does the first noise decide the outcome?) or do later decisions, which get fresh noise, wash it out?

    d. How we did it

    Pick statesReplay logged GEN episodes to their first alarm. 40 failed (population A) and 20 successful (C) states.
    P5.0: draw noiseFrom each state run 64 retries (A) or 8 (C), each with a fresh keyed noise.
    P5.1a: scoreFit a critic on deployment-legal features; test on whole held-out clusters.
    P5.1b: swap first noiseReuse a good or a bad draw's noise at the first decision only; later decisions get fresh noise.

    The plain version. Imagine the robot is stuck. Stop the clock at that moment, copy the situation, and let the robot try again 64 times, each time handing the policy a different random number. If some of those tries succeed, a lucky or clever choice would have saved the episode: that is how much room there is. Then ask whether anything the robot can see before it tries (the picture, the noise, the plan it produces) reveals which tries will work.

    P5.0 in detail: modes, keys, replay, estimators
    • Unit. The first accel alarm of a Part 3 GEN no-intervention episode. Population A = failed episodes (at most 8 per task); population C = succeeded ones. Each state is rebuilt by replaying the logged episode from reset; its decisions up to the alarm must equal the GEN log bitwise or the state is dropped.
    • Mode R (primary) is exactly Part 3's always-retry path: 10-step pause, arm-only rewind of 6 decisions with the return cost charged, one retry whose later decisions all use a fresh noise stream, no flag, no second retry, run to success or the 300-step budget. Mode C (secondary) re-plans the alarm decision with fresh noise and continues, with no pause and no rewind.
    • Keys. Fresh streams use namespace p5nc and branch index j inside the Part 2 key; no existing key changes (tested). Noise tensor shape (1, 50, 32) float32.
    • Controls. For 5 states the original GEN branches are re-run with their own keys and must equal the log (32 control branches matched exactly).
    • Estimators. Per state, p_s = n_s / K, and any-of-k = 1 - C(K - n_s, k) / C(K, k). Population means with a cluster bootstrap over (task, init), B = 10,000, percentile 95% intervals.
    • Budget cuts. The plan allowed a cut if the run projected over 10 h (the bench measured 7.3 s per branch in the worst case): A states 60 to 40, C retry draws 16 to 8, continue draws 32 to 16. Retry stayed at 64 draws.
    P5.1 in detail: features, candidates, evaluation, P5.1b
    • Feature sets: F0 = the 28 Part 3 alarm features (the state); F2 = the whole normalised chunk (50 x 7 = 50×7 = 350) plus summaries; F1 = the flattened first-decision noise (50×32 = 1,600) plus its norm; F3 = state plus plan.
    • Candidates, chosen by an inner grouped 4-fold cross-validation: logistic regression (L2, C = 0.05) on F3; gradient-boosted trees (depth 3, 150 trees, learning rate 0.05, subsample 0.8) on F3; logistic regression on F3 + F1. The inner cross-validation chose logistic F3 + F1 in 48 of 48 outer folds.
    • Evaluation: outer leave-one-(task, init)-cluster-out (35 clusters); scores are always out-of-fold. Primary metric: within-state AUROC over mixed states (states with both successful and failed draws). Secondary: best-of-8 gain (block of 8 draws, the critic keeps its favourite) over a random retry.
    • Which draws were analysed: 2,456 of 2,720 launched. Four A states had too little budget left for a retry, and one C state hit an invalid arm-only rewind (the restored arm penetrated an object by more than 1 cm).
    • P5.1b: 10 A states with a mixed outcome (0.1 ≤ p_s ≤ 0.9); for each, 4 good and 4 bad P5.0 draws are reused for the first decision's noise only, with 8 fresh streams for later decisions: 640 branches. Controls matched.
    • Permutation null (pre-registered): a fast stand-in model (logistic regression on 50 principal components). Because it is a different model from the primary critic, a post hoc null was added (see results).

    f. What it means, and what it does not show

    It means: the room exists, but we could not find a way to see it. A perfect noise picker would help a lot, and the first decision's noise carries part of the effect, but no feature of the observation, the noise or the plan identified the good noise at this data size (40 situations in 35 clusters). So a noise-steering policy would have to discover the signal online, by trial, instead of reading it off the situation.

    It does not show: that the signal cannot exist: NO-GO means "no signal at this data size", not "impossible"; that DSRL would fail (no actor was built and no reinforcement learning ran); an episode-level gain (the 8.98 pp figure is a ceiling for a perfect picker); that the per-task numbers are reliable (3 to 5 states each); or anything about TEST (no TEST episode was run). The situations that never succeed in 64 draws are out of reach of any noise picker and mark where residual or better-policy methods would have to work.

    g. Check yourself

    1. Why is "best of 64" an upper bound and not an expected gain?

    It assumes you can identify the winning draw for free, and selecting the maximum of many noisy outcomes overstates what is available from the situation alone. It bounds a perfect picker, not what a real one can reach.

    2. The critic's within-state AUROC is 0.552 and the post hoc p-value is 0.00999. Why is the verdict still NO-GO?

    Statistical significance and usefulness are different. The pre-registered GO rule needed AUROC of at least 0.650 and a best-of-8 gain of at least 10.0 pp. The AUROC is only slightly above 0.5 and the gain is +2.4 pp: distinguishable from chance, far too weak to use.

    3. Why compute AUROC within a situation instead of over all draws pooled?

    Pooling would reward telling easy situations from hard ones, which the deployed system cannot exploit at retry time (the situation is fixed). The question is which draw to pick for this situation.

    4. What do the "never recovered" situations (40.0%) tell you about the limits of noise steering?

    That for those situations no starting noise leads to success: the failure is a habit of the policy, not bad luck. No noise picker, however good, can fix them. They mark the domain of other methods.

    The baseline arm: SmolVLA with no intervention.
    patch
    A 14 x 14 pixel square. A 224 x 224 image becomes a 16 x 16 grid of patches, each described by a 384-number vector.
    pause
    After an alarm the robot holds still for 10 steps (0.5 s) so the scene settles before the camera check.
    permutation test
    Shuffle the labels many times, recompute the statistic, and see how often chance does as well as the real result.
    pp
    Percentage points: the difference between two percentages (65.0% minus 67.9% is -2.9 pp).
    pre-registration
    Writing the hypotheses, the analysis and the decision rules down and committing them before the data they judge exist.
    recall
    Of the cases that really are positive, the share the detector catches.
    rescue
    An episode the baseline P0 fails that the arm finishes.
    return cost
    The steps charged for a rewind: (alarm decision minus target decision) x 10 = 60 steps for a 6-decision rewind.
    rewind
    Restore only the arm (and its controller) to where it was 6 decisions (60 steps) earlier. Objects stay where they are. The 60 steps are charged to the budget.
    sealed set
    A test set (test_policy2) made and hashed before the model was chosen and evaluated exactly once, enforced by code.
    Siamese
    The same network (shared weights) applied to two inputs, here the before image and the after image, so their features can be compared.
    silent failure
    An episode that fails without the robot flagging a human. The project's own metric, always read with flag precision.
    SmolVLA
    A small open-source VLA policy. Here: the checkpoint HuggingFaceVLA/smolvla_libero, used frozen and never retrained.
    smooth max
    A log-mean-exp over patch scores with r = 5. Between the mean (dilutes a small change) and the max (noisy).
    static mask
    A fixed set of 81 of the 256 patches where a changed object never appeared in the training data. Model C zeroes them so the model cannot use background shortcuts.
    T-learner
    Two separate models: one predicts success if we retry, the other if we continue. Retry when the first beats the second by a margin.
    threshold
    The cut-off applied to a score to turn it into a yes/no decision.
    upper bound
    The best a method could do if one hard part were solved perfectly. Not a forecast.
    ViT
    Vision Transformer: splits an image into patches and processes them with attention.
    VLA
    Vision-language-action model: a neural network that reads camera images and a text instruction and outputs robot actions.
    within-state AUROC
    For one situation, the chance that a successful draw is scored above a failed draw by the critic. 0.5 means no information.
