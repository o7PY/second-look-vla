A study in robot recovery

Second Look

Before a robot retries, it takes a second look.

When a robot’s AI stumbles, should it simply try again?

The Project

A robot that checks before it retries

Robots driven by AI models (called vision-language-action policies, or VLAs) sometimes fail halfway through a job: they knock something over, drop it, or get stuck. The obvious fix is to try again. But if the failure moved something, trying again from a changed scene can make things worse.

Our aim

Can the robot notice that something is going wrong, early enough to act?An alarm built from the policy’s own behaviour.
Can it check what changed, using only a camera?A detector called DeltaDINO compares a before and an after photo.
Can it then pick the right response?Retry, carry on, or flag a human.

Our setup

Alarm, pause, look, decide

Click a step, or just watch. This loop is what every experiment builds on.

The robot’s brain

SmolVLA, a small open-source policy that reads camera images and an instruction and outputs arm movements.

The benchmark

LIBERO-Goal: one simulated tabletop scene with 10 different goals. No real robot. A good fit because its demonstrations record the simulator’s exact state at every step, so any moment can be restored and disturbed with a known true answer, and a SmolVLA policy for it already exists.

The rule

One budget of 300 steps. Pauses and rewinds are paid out of it, so a retry always costs something.

The data we made

Teaching a detector what “changed” looks like

We took 500 recorded demonstrations and scripted disturbances on top of them. For every one we saved a before photo and an after photo. The simulator knows every object’s exact position, so it gave us the true answer, but it is only used as the label, never as input to the detector.

60,565labelled before/after pairs

Try it: how the true label is decided

Changed: more than 1.0 cm or 5.0°. Unchanged: under 0.5 cm and 2.5°.

What we found

Three questions, three honest answers

Can the robot notice that something is going wrong, early enough to act? Yes. The alarm caught 86.1% of failing runs before time ran out, and it fired in 9.1% of runs that succeeded anyway (the threshold aimed for 10%).
Can it check what changed, using only a camera? Yes, and accurately. DeltaDINO’s AUROC is 0.999 on its test set (model C) and 0.989 inside the loop. But a change often just means the robot made progress, and the camera did not improve the retry decision: replacing it with the simulator’s own truth gave the same success (65.0% either way), and the retry model did no better with the camera score.
Can it pick the right response? Partly. Flagging roughly halves silent failures (32.1% to 15.4%) but costs 2.9 pp of success. Retrying once at the alarm helps a little: always retrying scored 70.8% against 68.5% for doing nothing, and the learned chooser gained +2.1 pp (a pre-registered test). But the chooser was no better than simply always retrying (−0.2 pp). Good retry noise exists (the best of 64 draws would rescue 60.0% of failures against 28.3% for one retry, an upper bound), but nothing we tried could pick it in advance (AUROC 0.552).
